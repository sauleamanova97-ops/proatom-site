Climate Change's Impact on Nuclear Power Plants

Between 2010 and 2019, approximately 4,000 weather-related disruptions affected nuclear power plants globally, primarily due to increased cooling water temperatures. A recent example from France was highlighted. This…

Between 2010 and 2019, approximately 4,000 weather-related disruptions affected nuclear power plants globally, primarily due to increased cooling water temperatures. A recent example from France was highlighted. This represents a threefold increase compared to 1990–2009, resulting in nearly 50 TWh of lost electricity generation, according to research published in *Energy Economics*. In Europe, an hourly power outage costs approximately €17.10 per kilowatt-hour, with indirect costs, such as infrastructure disruptions, potentially exceeding direct costs by about 50%. Nuclear power plant operators are proactively scheduling maintenance during droughts, indicating an adaptation to gradual and predictable extreme weather events to mitigate the risk of more expensive unscheduled outages. Transitioning to a closed-loop cooling system could offer a solution, but it would cost around $500 million per nuclear power plant. Another option proposed is to relax environmental restrictions on river temperatures, which would allow reactors to operate during heatwaves.

Kazakhstan's National Nuclear Center (NNC RK) is conducting tests on new structural materials for nuclear energy, a process that can take 10 to 15 years. Yerzhan Sapataev, head of the Radiation Materials Science Laboratory at the Institute of Atomic Energy, a branch of the NNC RK, stated that reactor materials are exposed to high temperatures (from 500 to 1000 °C and above), intense neutron irradiation that alters metal structures, and aggressive coolants such as liquid sodium, lead-bismuth, or high-temperature helium. Samples of ferritic-martensitic and austenitic steels, along with oxide dispersion-strengthened materials, are placed in the core to simulate actual neutron radiation spectra and thermal loads. The IGR pulsed graphite reactor is used to study material behavior during transient and accident scenarios, including core meltdown, by creating short, high-power pulses. Phasing out Russian uranium would necessitate multi-billion dollar investments in new supply chains.

Low water levels on the Danube have idled two major regional nuclear power plants. Romania and Hungary are employing different methods to stabilize their energy systems, offering valuable insights for Kazakhstan. At Romania's Cernavodă Nuclear Power Plant, which operates two CANDU reactors of approximately 700 MW each, the situation escalated to a special operation. Unit 1 was shut down, and the water intake for Unit 2 was threatened. To save the plant, military and sapper teams detonated 180 kg of explosives to clear underwater rock in the Danube, redirecting water flow from the Bal arm directly to the plant's intake systems. Hungary opted for a complete reduction in output. The Paks Nuclear Power Plant, featuring four Soviet VVER-440 units, gradually decreased its power from 2000 MW to zero. The core issue was identical: open-cycle pumping stations were physically unable to draw from the shallow and overheated river water. What does this mean for Kazakhstan? Kazakhstan has selected Rosatom's VVER-1200 design for its first nuclear power plant in Ulken village. This raises the question: if river-based nuclear power plants in Europe are shutting down one after another, could a similar collapse occur on the shrinking Lake Balkhash? Firstly, the cooling type differs. Europe's Paks and Cernavodă are older plants with once-through cooling, requiring a massive, constant flow of water directly from the river. The Kazakh project incorporates a closed-loop cooling system with cooling towers. Water circulates in a closed circuit, with only a minimal volume drawn from Balkhash to compensate for evaporation (approximately 63 million cubic meters per year for two units, representing only 0.33% of the lake's natural evaporation). Unlike Romania, Kazakhstan will not depend on a 'flowing stream'. For Kazakhstan, the threat manifests differently. Balkhash is an endorheic lake, fed by the Ili River (whose flow is partly controlled by China). During a prolonged drought, the lake's level declines over years, not days. The primary risk is the physical retreat of the shoreline near Ulken village by hundreds of meters, complicating the operation of make-up water systems. The European crisis underscores the need to design pumping stations and supply channels for Balkhash with a giant depth margin, accounting for the most pessimistic climate scenarios and lake level drops for 50 years into the future. What's in the Multibillion-Dollar US-Saudi Nuclear Deal Beyond Uranium Enrichment?

Potential uranium enrichment is just the most politically charged headline in the so-called "123 Agreement." The broader infrastructure program spans three decades as part of Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 strategy.

Beyond building a fuel fabrication plant, the deal covers power generation — most of the funding will go toward building commercial nuclear plants of American design. Westinghouse is the leading contender for the contracts, putting the company in a strong position. Given the desert climate, the reactors are also planned to power large desalination plants, addressing the kingdom's water security concerns. The agreement effectively locks Washington's main competitors — Rosatom and China's CNNC — out of the Saudi market. American companies gain contracts for equipment supply, safety systems, servicing, and workforce training. Source: US Department of Energy

Saudi Arabia May Be Allowed to Enrich Uranium on Its Own Soil

The decision was announced in Washington as part of a nuclear agreement with Riyadh — raising concerns about the risk of an arms race in the Middle East.

Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman has previously stated plainly that if Iran builds a nuclear bomb, Saudi Arabia will do the same. Unlike a similar 2009 deal with the UAE, where Abu Dhabi gave up domestic enrichment entirely, Riyadh negotiated for maximum technological sovereignty. The multibillion-dollar project is formalized in what's known as the "123 Agreement," signed by US Energy Secretary Chris Wright and his Saudi counterpart, Abdulaziz bin Salman. The document lays the legal groundwork for decades of cooperation and opens the Saudi market to American nuclear companies. The US Department of Energy says the agreement meets the highest standards of safety and non-proliferation. The document now goes to Congress: if Congress doesn't vote it down within 90 days, the agreement takes effect. Experts estimate the kingdom's first civilian nuclear plants are unlikely to appear for at least another decade. Source: BBC
