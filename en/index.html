The IAEA's "Energy, Electricity and Nuclear Power Estimates for the Period up to 2060" report marks the sixth consecutive year the agency has raised its nuclear power capacity projections. It now expects global nuclear capacity to more than triple by 2060. As of the end of 2025, the world operated 413 nuclear power reactors with a total capacity of 377.1 GW(e). The IAEA's high growth scenario forecasts this figure could reach 1,045 GW(e) by 2050, up from last year's projection of 992 GW(e). This year's projections extend for the first time to 2060, by which point nuclear generation capacity is expected to reach 1,284 GW(e), a 3.4-fold increase from the end of 2025. Under a “low growth” scenario, nuclear capacity is projected to reach 696 GW(e) by 2060 and 641 GW(e) by 2050, compared to last year's 561 GW(e) forecast for 2050. The publication, prepared by an international group of experts, bases its estimates on all operational reactors, potential license extensions, planned shutdowns, and realistic and planned construction projects for the coming decades. The low growth scenario assumes current trends continue with minor changes in legislation, policy, and regulation. The high growth scenario considers national intentions to expand nuclear energy use, with the report noting that this projection “remains both realistic and technically feasible” and that actual growth could exceed this estimate. Achieving even the high growth scenario will require favorable conditions including national policies, investment support, human resource development, regulatory cooperation, and global harmonization of requirements. “The IAEA’s projections show the growing role of nuclear power in meeting the world’s rising electricity needs. Assuming an average capacity of 300 MW(e), the low growth scenario projects SMRs will account for 120 GW(e) by 2060, equivalent to 400 new units. If the average capacity is 250 MW(e), this would equate to 480 units. Last year’s forecast for 2050 estimated SMRs would comprise 24% in the high growth scenario and 5% in the low growth scenario. The IAEA's projections, extended to 2060 for the first time, include a regional breakdown for SMR deployment. Approximately 60% of new nuclear capacity in North America could come from SMRs. In Southeast Asia, Latin America, and the Caribbean, this figure is 40% in both high and low growth scenarios. Lifetime extensions for existing reactors are also crucial for achieving the projected growth in nuclear capacity.

Hungary has fully shut down its Paks Nuclear Power Plant, the country's main energy facility and source of nearly 50% of its electricity, due to critically low water levels in the Danube River caused by anomalous heat and drought in Europe. The decision marks the first such shutdown in 44 years. The plant's output dropped from 965 MW on Friday to 240 MW overnight, significantly below its normal 2000 MW capacity, prompting the shutdown. Operational regulations mandate a shutdown when the Danube's water level near Paks reaches -134 cm. Hydrologists now project a further drop to -144 cm, surpassing the previous historical low of -98 cm recorded in 2018. The plant's pumping systems are physically unable to draw the required volume of water. Hungary has already imposed strict water consumption limits in over 100 settlements and urged industries to reduce water intake. The drought's impact extends to logistics, tourism, and agriculture along the Danube. Amid potential energy shortages in Budapest, neighboring Slovakia, through Prime Minister Robert Fico, has stated it is monitoring the situation and is prepared to share emergency electricity flows. The company asserts its technology addresses the cost and scalability issues of nuclear power. Conventional water-cooled reactors operate at lower temperatures (below 300°C), necessitating bulky and expensive steam turbines, heat exchangers, and cooling systems. Gas turbines, used in aviation and gas-fired power plants, convert very hot air directly into electricity with minimal losses, making them compact and economical even at a small scale. However, they require much higher operating temperatures. Nuclear Turbines' new reactor directly heats compressed air to high temperatures using innovative materials, avoiding graphite, which combusts on contact with air. According to co-founder O'Ouston, their approach was to first identify the cheapest method of electricity generation and then design a reactor to match that method. The company has secured initial funding from IQ Capital, Rhapsody Venture Partners, Zero Carbon Capital, and Empirical Ventures.
