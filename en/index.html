British Startup Nuclear Turbines Unveils Compact Gas Turbine Reactor Concept

British company Nuclear Turbines, co-founded by former BAE Systems chief engineer Jeremy O'Ouston and University of Manchester professor Tim Abram, has developed a new reactor type that utilizes highly efficient…

British company Nuclear Turbines, co-founded by former BAE Systems chief engineer Jeremy O'Ouston and University of Manchester professor Tim Abram, has developed a new reactor type that utilizes highly efficient high-temperature gas turbines instead of traditional steam turbines. The company asserts its technology addresses the cost and scalability issues of nuclear power. Conventional water-cooled reactors operate at lower temperatures (below 300°C), necessitating bulky and expensive steam turbines, heat exchangers, and cooling systems. Gas turbines, used in aviation and gas-fired power plants, convert very hot air directly into electricity with minimal losses, making them compact and economical even at a small scale. However, they require much higher operating temperatures. Nuclear Turbines' new reactor directly heats compressed air to high temperatures using innovative materials, avoiding graphite, which combusts on contact with air. According to co-founder O'Ouston, their approach was to first identify the cheapest method of electricity generation and then design a reactor to match that method. The company has secured initial funding from IQ Capital, Rhapsody Venture Partners, Zero Carbon Capital, and Empirical Ventures. These funds will be used for refining and validating the reactor design, establishing large-scale test stands, and expanding the team to produce the first fuel elements. These reactors are envisioned for applications such as powering data centers, industrial facilities, and serving as components in microgrids and hybrid energy systems. Source: World Nuclear News

Port of Long Beach Joins U.S. Maritime SMR Initiative

The Port of Long Beach, one of the largest container ports in the U.S., signed a memorandum of cooperation with the U.S. Maritime Administration (MARAD) on July 22. This makes it the first American port to formalize a partnership under an initiative to develop small modular reactors (SMRs) for commercial shipping. MARAD launched the initiative in May to boost U.S. shipbuilding, lower costs, and strengthen the nation's energy independence. Under the agreement, the Port of Long Beach and MARAD will collaborate with the U.S. Coast Guard, the Department of Energy, and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission. The goal is to define operational protocols, safety standards, and inspection processes necessary for the safe entry and servicing of SMR-powered vessels in U.S. ports, as well as to develop and share best practices. The agreement is non-binding and does not involve funding or procurement decisions. Additionally, the Port of Long Beach has entered a new lease agreement with BlueCore Energy, a company developing small modular water-cooled reactors for installation on floating barges. These reactors are designed to power ports, critical infrastructure, and cargo vessel propulsion. BlueCore Energy has already delivered its first barge and an electric test reactor to its Port of Long Beach headquarters, where it is engaged in assembling, testing, and storing maritime energy modules. BlueCore Energy's initial 10 MW system can power approximately 15,000 homes or scale to meet the needs of a large port. The reactors are designed for multi-year operation without refueling and can be used to supply power to ports, utilities, data centers, and marine infrastructure.

Germany's Nuclear Energy Shift: Domestic Phase-Out vs. Imported Power

Germany's experience demonstrates that while a country can decommission its nuclear power plants, it may not be able to eliminate the energy they produce. In 2015, Germany generated 91,790 GWh from nuclear sources.…

Germany's experience demonstrates that while a country can decommission its nuclear power plants, it may not be able to eliminate the energy they produce. In 2015, Germany generated 91,790 GWh from nuclear sources. Following the complete phase-out of its reactors, this figure dropped to zero in 2024. Over the same period, the volume of imported nuclear-generated electricity more than tripled, reaching 18,313 GWh in 2024. This indicates that phasing out domestic nuclear generation does not necessarily remove nuclear power from the country's electricity consumption mix.
